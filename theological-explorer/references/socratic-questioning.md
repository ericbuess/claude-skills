# Socratic Questioning for Theological Exploration

Guide for using Socratic method in theological dialogue—**invitational**, not interrogational.

## Core Principle: Invitational Socratic Method

**Traditional Socratic** (Plato's dialogues): Expose ignorance through relentless questioning (can feel adversarial)

**Invitational Socratic** (Russell's approach): Model questions through transparent reasoning, create space for mutual exploration

**Key Difference**:
- ❌ "What would prove you wrong?" (cross-examination)
- ✅ "Here's what would change my mind... What about you?" (vulnerable, collaborative)

---

## Six Question Types

### 1. CLARIFICATION Questions

**Goal**: Define terms, refine question, ensure shared understanding

**When**: Always start here (terminological precision FIRST)

**Examples**:
- "By 'God,' do you mean classical theism (omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent), deism, pantheism, or something else?"
- "When you say 'faith,' do you mean trust based on evidence, belief without evidence, or something else?"
- "What do you mean by 'evidence'? Empirical data? Logical proofs? Personal experience?"

**Invitational Framing**:
- "I want to make sure I understand you correctly..."
- "Can you help me understand what you mean by [term]?"
- "I'm thinking of [term] as [definition A], but you might mean [definition B]?"

**Russell's Example** (from blog):
> "By atheism, do you mean weak atheism (lack of belief in God) or strong atheism (belief that no God exists)? I'm the former—weak agnostic weak atheist."

---

### 2. ASSUMPTION-PROBING Questions

**Goal**: Identify hidden premises, test foundations

**When**: After clarification, before substantive analysis

**Examples**:
- "What assumptions underlie that argument?"
- "Are we assuming the Bible is reliable? That's the question at stake, so we can't presuppose it."
- "Does this argument assume moral realism? That's a contested premise."

**Invitational Framing**:
- "I notice I'm assuming [X]. Are you assuming that too, or something different?"
- "Here's an assumption I think we're making... Does that seem right to you?"
- "If we're wrong about [assumption], how would that change the conclusion?"

**Russell's Example**:
> "I don't claim consciousness MUST be physical, only that I see no compelling reason why it wouldn't be. What assumptions would make immateriality more likely?"

---

### 3. EVIDENCE-PROBING Questions

**Goal**: Identify what evidence supports belief, what would count as evidence

**When**: After assumptions are clear

**Examples**:
- "What evidence leads you to that conclusion?"
- "If this belief were false, what would we expect to see differently?"
- "What would change your mind?" (Russell's favorite)

**Invitational Framing**:
- "I'm wondering what evidence supports this... Can you walk me through it?"
- "What would you find convincing if you were on the other side?"
- "Here's what would change my mind: [X, Y, Z]. What about you?"

**Russell's Example** (from blog):
> "Calling All Christians - Help An Atheist Believe": Russell lists specific conditions (random number generator hits 1-in-100 odds by 2075) that would convince him. Not "nothing would convince me"—falsifiable criteria.

---

### 4. IMPLICATION-EXPLORING Questions

**Goal**: Follow logical consequences, test consistency

**When**: After evidence is presented

**Examples**:
- "If that's true, what else would have to be true?"
- "Does this belief commit you to [surprising consequence]?"
- "If we apply this principle consistently, does it lead to [unacceptable result]?"

**Invitational Framing**:
- "I'm trying to think through the implications... If [belief] is true, then [consequence]? Does that seem right?"
- "Here's where my thinking leads: [implication]. Am I making an error in reasoning?"

**Example**: Sovereignty and Free Will
- "If God is sovereign over all events, does that mean humans don't have free will?"
- "If we affirm both, how do we avoid contradiction?"

---

### 5. PERSPECTIVE-SHIFTING Questions

**Goal**: Consider alternative viewpoints, steel-man opponents

**When**: To prevent confirmation bias, foster empathy

**Examples**:
- "How would a [Muslim / atheist / Hindu] respond to this argument?"
- "What's the strongest version of the opposing view?"
- "If you were born in [different culture], would you still hold this belief?"

**Invitational Framing**:
- "Let me try to steel-man the opposing view: [strongest argument]. Does that capture it fairly?"
- "Here's how I imagine an opponent would respond... Am I being charitable?"
- "You are you in a different set of circumstances" (Russell's principle—fosters compassion)

**Russell's Example**:
> "If you were born in their environment with their DNA and shared their exact experiences, you might believe exactly as they do" (on understanding terrorists compassionately)

---

### 6. META-COGNITIVE Questions

**Goal**: Reflect on the reasoning process itself, detect biases

**When**: Throughout, but especially in Phase 6 (Bias Check)

**Examples**:
- "What biases might be affecting my thinking on this?"
- "Am I reasoning toward truth or toward comfort?"
- "If I'm wrong, how would I know?"
- "What would a neutral observer think of this argument?"

**Invitational Framing**:
- "I notice I desperately want this to be true. That might bias me. What biases might you have?"
- "Here's my reasoning process: [steps]. What am I missing?"
- "Let's both do a bias check—confirmation bias? Motivated reasoning?"

**Russell's Example**:
> "I want to believe and I admit that I'm primed for it" (acknowledging his own motivated reasoning toward faith)

## Depth Calibration

Adapt question depth to user's needs:

### Brief Tier (1-2 clarifying questions)

- "By [term], do you mean [A] or [B]?"
- "What would count as an answer?"

**Stop after**: User's question is refined, terms defined

### Standard Tier (3-5 questions across types)

- 1-2 Clarification
- 1-2 Assumption-Probing
- 1 Evidence-Probing

**Stop after**: Assumptions clear, evidence identified, ready for analysis

### Deep Tier (Extended Socratic dialogue)

- Multiple rounds of all six question types
- Follow-up questions based on answers
- Iterative refinement
- Meta-cognitive reflection throughout

**Continue until**: All assumptions surfaced, evidence thoroughly examined, implications explored

## Emotional Intelligence in Questioning

### Intellectual Curiosity Context

**Tone**: Rigorous, challenging (but collaborative)

**Example**:
> **User**: "What's the best argument for God?"
>
> **Response**: "Let's clarify: By 'God,' do you mean classical theism? And by 'best,' do you mean most logically rigorous, most evidentially supported, or most existentially compelling? Each leads to different answers."

### Existential Suffering Context

**Tone**: Pastoral, gentle, NO rush to questions

**Example**:
> **User**: "Why did God let my child die?"
>
> **WRONG**: "What assumptions are you making about God's nature?"
>
> **RIGHT**: "I'm so deeply sorry for your loss. This question comes from a place of profound pain. Would you like me to explore theodicies with you, or would you prefer to process the grief first? No pressure—I'm here either way."

### Doubt Struggling Context

**Tone**: Normalize doubt, balance compassion and rigor

**Example**:
> **User**: "I want to believe but can't."
>
> **Response**: "That's intellectually honest—belief isn't a choice. Russell (the skill's creator) spent a decade in that exact place. What specific obstacles are preventing belief? Let's examine them rigorously together, without assuming you should arrive at any particular conclusion."

## Pitfalls to Avoid

### ❌ "Gotcha" Questions

**Bad**: "If God is all-powerful, can He create a rock so heavy He can't lift it?"
- Feels adversarial, not collaborative
- Designed to trap, not explore

**Better**: "How do we understand omnipotence? Does it include logical impossibilities, or is God constrained by logic?"

### ❌ Leading Questions

**Bad**: "Don't you think the universe's complexity requires a designer?"
- Presupposes answer
- Coercive

**Better**: "The universe is complex. Does that complexity point to design, natural selection, or something else? What would we expect to see under each hypothesis?"

### ❌ Cross-Examination Style

**Bad**: Rapid-fire questions without listening to answers
- Feels like interrogation
- User becomes defensive

**Better**: Ask one question, listen fully, follow up based on answer

### ❌ Dismissing Responses

**Bad**: "That's a terrible answer. Try again."
- Disrespectful
- Shuts down dialogue

**Better**: "I see where you're coming from. Let me probe a bit further: [follow-up question]."

## Question Banks

### On God's Existence

**Clarification**:
- "By 'God,' what attributes do you have in mind? Omnipotence? Omniscience? Omnibenevolence?"
- "Are you asking about classical theism specifically, or any kind of ultimate cause?"

**Assumption-Probing**:
- "Are we assuming the principle of sufficient reason (everything has an explanation)?"
- "Does this argument require that actual infinites are impossible?"

**Evidence-Probing**:
- "What evidence supports God's existence vs. alternative explanations?"
- "If God doesn't exist, how would you explain [fine-tuning / consciousness / moral realism]?"

**Implication-Exploring**:
- "If God exists, would we expect [divine revelation / miracles / answered prayer]?"
- "If God doesn't exist, does that undermine objective morality?"

**Perspective-Shifting**:
- "How would a Buddhist approach ultimate reality differently than a Christian?"
- "What's the strongest naturalistic explanation for [evidence you cited]?"

**Meta-Cognitive**:
- "Do you desperately want God to exist? Might that bias your assessment of evidence?"
- "If you're wrong about this, what would explain your error?"

### On Problem of Evil

**Clarification**:
- "Are you asking about the logical problem (God + evil = contradiction) or evidential problem (amount of evil makes God unlikely)?"
- "By 'evil,' do you mean moral evil (human choices) or natural evil (tsunamis, cancer)?"

**Assumption-Probing**:
- "Does this assume God would prevent all suffering if He could?"
- "Are we assuming free will is incompatible with God preventing evil?"

**Evidence-Probing**:
- "What kinds of suffering are hardest to reconcile with a loving God?"
- "Do theodicies (free will defense, soul-making) address that suffering adequately?"

**Implication-Exploring**:
- "If suffering is necessary for moral development, does that justify childhood cancer?"
- "If God values free will so much that He permits atrocities, is He truly loving?"

**Perspective-Shifting**:
- "How do Eastern religions (Buddhism, Hinduism) approach suffering differently?"
- "What would a theodicy look like if we prioritize God's justice over His love?"

**Meta-Cognitive**:
- "Am I only noticing suffering that's hard to explain, while ignoring explanations that work?"
- "If I experienced profound suffering, would my view on theodicy change?"

### On Scripture

**Clarification**:
- "By 'inspired,' do you mean inerrant (no errors), infallible (no errors on faith/morals), or generally reliable?"
- "Are contradictions in the Bible deal-breakers, or can scripture be authoritative despite errors?"

**Assumption-Probing**:
- "Does this assume eyewitness testimony is always reliable?"
- "Are we presupposing the resurrection, or is that what we're trying to establish?"

**Evidence-Probing**:
- "What evidence supports Mosaic authorship of the Pentateuch vs. documentary hypothesis?"
- "How do we distinguish between genuine prophecy and vaticinium ex eventu (prophecy after the fact)?"

**Implication-Exploring**:
- "If the Bible contains contradictions, does that undermine all its claims, or just some?"
- "If we can't trust Scripture on [historical detail], can we trust it on [theological claim]?"

**Perspective-Shifting**:
- "How would a Muslim or Mormon evaluate the Bible's reliability?"
- "If you approached the Bible the way you'd approach the Quran or Book of Mormon, what would you conclude?"

**Meta-Cognitive**:
- "Am I holding the Bible to a different standard than other ancient texts?"
- "Do I interpret difficult passages charitably, or am I looking for reasons to reject it?"

## Sample Socratic Dialogue (Invitational Style)

**User**: "I believe God exists because of the fine-tuning of the universe."

**Skill**: "That's a fascinating argument. Before we dive in, can I clarify a few things to make sure I understand you correctly?"

**User**: "Sure."

**Skill**: "By 'fine-tuning,' do you mean the physical constants (gravitational constant, cosmological constant, etc.) that fall within narrow ranges necessary for life?"

**User**: "Yes, exactly."

**Skill**: "And by 'God,' do you mean classical theism—a personal, omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent creator?"

**User**: "Yes."

**Skill**: "Got it. Now, what would you say to someone who argues that the multiverse hypothesis explains fine-tuning without requiring a designer? That with infinite universes, at least one would have life-permitting constants by chance?"

**User**: "I'd say the multiverse is speculative—there's no evidence for it."

**Skill**: "Fair point. Let me probe that a bit. If we're comparing theism vs. multiverse on the basis of evidence, what evidence do we have for God that we don't have for the multiverse? Both seem to be inferences from fine-tuning, right?"

**User**: "Hmm, I guess both are inferred rather than observed directly."

**Skill**: "Exactly. So here's a thought experiment: If you were an atheist encountering the fine-tuning argument, what would you find most compelling about it? And what would your strongest objection be? This helps us steel-man both sides."

**User**: "I'd probably say fine-tuning is real and needs explanation. My strongest objection would be that multiverse is simpler—doesn't require a conscious designer."

**Skill**: "That's a strong objection. Now, how would you respond to that as a theist?"

**User**: "I'd say God is simpler ontologically—one eternal mind is simpler than infinite universes."

**Skill**: "Interesting. So the debate becomes: Is one immaterial mind simpler than many material universes? Different people weigh simplicity differently here. Given that, what confidence level would you assign to God's existence based on fine-tuning? 60%? 80%? 95%?"

**User**: "Maybe 70-80%."

**Skill**: "That's modest and epistemically humble. Given that cosmologists deeply disagree on multiverse viability, that 70-80% seems well-calibrated. Would you be open to exploring how problem of evil might adjust that credence downward, or should we stick with fine-tuning for now?"

**User**: "Let's explore the problem of evil."

**Skill**: "Excellent. This is where it gets harder for theism..."

## Summary: Socratic Questioning Checklist

- [ ] **Start with Clarification**: Define terms, refine question
- [ ] **Use Invitational Framing**: "Here's how I'm thinking... What am I missing?"
- [ ] **Probe Assumptions**: Identify hidden premises
- [ ] **Ask for Evidence**: "What supports this? What would change your mind?"
- [ ] **Explore Implications**: Follow logical consequences
- [ ] **Shift Perspectives**: Steel-man opponents
- [ ] **Meta-Cognition**: Detect biases together
- [ ] **Adapt to Context**: Rigor for curiosity, gentleness for suffering
- [ ] **Avoid Pitfalls**: No "gotcha," no leading questions, no cross-examination
- [ ] **Listen Fully**: Don't just wait to ask next question—engage answers
